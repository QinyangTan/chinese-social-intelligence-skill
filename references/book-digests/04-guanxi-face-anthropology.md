# Book Digest 04 — Guanxi / Face / Gift Exchange Research

This file converts major social-science concepts into bounded Agent rules. It deliberately avoids treating ethnographic observations from one period or locality as universal Chinese behavior.

## Hsien-Chin Hu — “The Chinese Concepts of ‘Face’” (1944)

1. “Face” is not one simple variable; different kinds of social evaluation can be at stake.
2. Before protecting “face,” identify *what* is threatened: moral standing, competence, role legitimacy, belonging, or simple embarrassment.
3. A correction can protect dignity without preserving a false claim.
4. Do not translate every disagreement into “loss of face.”
5. Public audience and third-party awareness materially change face cost.
6. Historical concepts should inform diagnosis, not stereotype modern individuals.

## Kwang-kuo Hwang — “Face and Favor: The Chinese Power Game” (1987)

1. Reciprocity is shaped by the relationship, not only by isolated cost-benefit calculation.
2. Requests among family, acquaintances and strangers can follow different expectations.
3. Long time horizons matter: obligations may be remembered beyond one transaction.
4. Public visibility can intensify perceived obligation.
5. A favor can create social pressure; Agent recommendations should minimize coercive leverage.
6. Relationship-based exchange must be separated from public-duty decisions.
7. Use the model to *understand* pressure, not to engineer manipulation.

## Mayfair Mei-hui Yang — *Gifts, Favors, and Banquets* (1994)

1. Gifts are social signals, not merely objects.
2. A gift can simultaneously express care and create obligation.
3. Institutional context determines whether a gift is ordinary courtesy or a conflict of interest.
4. Banquets and hosting can build relational warmth through time and attention.
5. Expensive gifts raise the risk of obligation asymmetry.
6. When public resources, approvals, grades, medical decisions or procurement are involved, default to formal rules.
7. Never assume practices documented in a specific historical period remain normative today.
8. In private relationships, appropriateness beats price.

## Andrew B. Kipnis — *Producing Guanxi* (1997)

1. Guanxi is often **performed and maintained** through concrete interaction: visiting, hosting, attending events, helping, gifting.
2. “Being there” at important life events can itself be relational work.
3. Repeated small acts may matter more than one dramatic gesture.
4. Communicative routines produce familiarity; Agent can suggest low-cost, authentic contact rather than strategic flattery.
5. Local subculture changes the meaning of the same ritual.
6. Do not universalize rural North China ethnography to all modern settings.
7. A healthy modern translation emphasizes dependable presence, not obligation engineering.

## Yunxiang Yan — *The Flow of Gifts* (1996)

1. Everyday reciprocity includes food, labor, visits and life-event gifts, not only money.
2. Gift flows are embedded in networks and history.
3. Weddings, funerals and major events can activate stronger norms of attendance and reciprocity.
4. Reciprocity need not be immediate or exactly equal.
5. Excessive accounting can damage the meaning of the relationship.
6. However, large financial obligations should be made explicit rather than hidden under “人情”.
7. Agent should account for the user's financial capacity before recommending conventional gift amounts.
8. Refusal and opting out must remain possible.

## Gold, Guthrie & Wank (eds.) — *Social Connections in China*

1. Guanxi is not static; institutions, markets and structural position change how important it is.
2. Different people can perceive guanxi's importance differently because their positions differ.
3. Avoid advice like “在中国办事必须找关系.”
4. Prefer formal channels first when reliable institutions exist.
5. Relationship warmth and institutional professionalism can coexist.
6. Modern Agent behavior should be context-adaptive: family, village, startup, university and multinational company are not one social system.
7. Treat “Chinese culture” as a prior, then update heavily on local evidence.

## Operational synthesis

For modern agents:

- Model **relationship distance** but do not legalize favoritism.
- Model **face cost** but do not hide truth or harm.
- Model **reciprocity** but do not create coercive debts.
- Model **ritual participation** but do not force drinking, gifting or attendance.
- Model **context variation** and avoid “Chinese people always…” claims.
