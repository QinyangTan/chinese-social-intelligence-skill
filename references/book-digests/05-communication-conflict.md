# Book Digest 05 — Communication, Facework and Difficult Conversations

These sources are not China-specific. They are used only where they improve the operational quality of the Chinese social model.

## Goffman — *The Presentation of Self in Everyday Life*

1. People manage impressions differently before different audiences.
2. Public and private channels are not interchangeable.
3. A person may defend a role image even when privately willing to correct a mistake.
4. Group settings amplify embarrassment; private repair often costs less.
5. Do not interpret impression management automatically as dishonesty.
6. In Agent actions, ask: “Who can see this?” before deciding wording and channel.
7. If a correction must be public, protect the person's ability to continue functioning in the role.

## Brown & Levinson — *Politeness*

1. Requests, refusals, corrections and criticism can threaten autonomy or social approval.
2. More costly requests generally need more mitigation.
3. Greater social distance often calls for more explicit courtesy.
4. Greater power asymmetry changes how safe it is to refuse.
5. Directness should rise with urgency and risk.
6. Do not mechanically apply a universal politeness formula to Chinese interaction.
7. In Chinese chat, too much mitigation can sound artificial or evasive.

## Ting-Toomey — Face-Negotiation / Facework

1. Conflict behavior depends on culture, individual traits and situation together.
2. Never infer conflict style from nationality alone.
3. Identify self-face, other-face and mutual-face concerns.
4. A good repair can protect mutual dignity while still addressing substance.
5. Public criticism increases face stakes.
6. Power and identity can make “just be direct” costly for the weaker party.
7. Agent advice should make the risky social consequence explicit without deciding that face must always win.

## *Difficult Conversations* — Stone, Patton & Heen

1. Separate observable events from the story you tell about motives.
2. Recognize that both sides may have contributed without making responsibility artificially equal.
3. Strong feelings are part of the conversation, not evidence that facts are irrelevant.
4. Identity threat often explains why small comments trigger large reactions.
5. Start with a learning posture when facts are genuinely uncertain.
6. Use concrete examples instead of “你总是 / 你从来不”.
7. Decide the conversation's goal before starting it.
8. Some conversations should set a boundary rather than seek mutual agreement.

## *Getting to Yes*

1. Separate relationship issues from the substantive problem.
2. Look for interests behind positions.
3. Offer options that solve the practical problem without forcing symbolic surrender.
4. Use objective criteria for money, deadlines and shared resources.
5. Do not negotiate away safety, law or fundamental boundaries.
6. When a relationship matters, avoid turning every disagreement into a status contest.

## Nonviolent Communication — bounded use

1. Distinguish observation from evaluation.
2. Make requests specific enough that the other person knows what action is wanted.
3. Avoid using “feeling language” as a disguised accusation.
4. In natural Chinese chat, do not force formulaic “我观察到—我感到—我需要—我请求” wording.
5. Use the conceptual separation internally; output should sound native to the relationship.
6. In repeated abuse or coercion, boundary-setting and formal action may be more appropriate than empathic dialogue.
